\section{Introduction}

Diffusion transformers generate a video by iterating a denoiser fifty times, and classifier-free guidance doubles each iteration into a conditional and an unconditional pass, so a single clip costs a hundred forward passes. Training-free caching reduces this cost without touching the weights. A subset of the steps is evaluated exactly, and the remaining steps reuse something that was computed earlier. TeaCache, TaylorSeer, RA-CFGCache and the sensitivity-based caches that followed all follow this recipe, and they differ in two decisions that the literature has largely kept apart: where to place the exact calls, and what a skipped step should return.

The first decision is usually made by a threshold on a quantity that has already been observed. TeaCache accumulates the change of the modulated input and skips while that change stays under a level, RA-CFGCache composes a risk from the two guidance branches and compares it to a calibrated table, and the sensitivity-aware caches calibrate a per-timestep quantity and threshold it. Each of these is a rule for the second decision applied at every step, which makes the placement local in time.

We separated the two decisions and measured each at a matched number of transformer calls. The cost of skipping is positional and steep. On CogVideoX-2B, skipping the first step costs 0.57 LPIPS against the full run and the second 0.45, while every step after the tenth costs about 0.04, so the head carries about three quarters of the sensitivity and the tail is nearly flat. A schedule that holds sixteen of fifty steps exact and spreads the remaining six along the tail therefore beats a uniform grid, which is the zero-head limit of the same family, by five to six times at tight budgets. The profile that produces this schedule is a property of the sampler: two prompts rank the first eight steps identically and correlate at 0.94.

The second decision turned out to be worth a quarter of the remaining error, and it has a law. Reusing the last exact output is what every cache in this literature does. Adding a damped part of the difference between the last two exact outputs instead, with a damping of about one half, lowers LPIPS from 0.156 to 0.108 at the same cost. What does not work is the natural generalisation: scaling the same difference by the number of steps since the anchor gives 0.442, because it extrapolates a curvature that the two stored outputs do not determine. That is the choice a first-order Taylor predictor makes in feature space, and it is why such a predictor collapses on this backbone while a damped correction in output space does not.

Combining the two gives the method we evaluate. It needs one measurement per sampler, no calibration set, no learned component, and it leaves the solver and the weights untouched. At the setting the baseline papers report, one hundred prompts at forty-nine frames, it reaches 0.130 LPIPS against 0.155 for the risk-based cache of RA-CFGCache and 0.153 against 0.169 on Wan2.1 at eighty-one frames, first on SSIM and PSNR in both. We also report what did not help. Re-planning the placement at every step, the model-predictive reading of the same problem, cannot change a fixed-budget allocation when the model supplies a single global error scale, and three implementations confirm it. A per-step error model that might certify a schedule does not add up. The sum of the single-skip costs explains 59\% of the variance of the realised loss and is exceeded by 29\% of the samples, so an additive certificate is unavailable.
